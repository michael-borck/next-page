The rule: new things go on the next page

On a flip notebook, tomorrow is one lift away. So when something new turns up today, you lift the page and write it on tomorrow’s. Today’s list stays as you planned it, with a line drawn underneath. Mark Forster calls this a closed list.

Four things follow from that one habit.

  • You can finish today. The list doesn’t grow while you work, so getting to the end of it means something.
  • Interruptions wait a day. Most things that feel urgent are fine tomorrow. Writing them down tomorrow makes you check.
  • Planning is already done. By evening, tomorrow’s list is mostly written. You never rewrite a list from scratch.
  • You find out early when you’ve taken on too much. If you can’t finish the list three days running, the problem is how much you’ve agreed to, and you know it within a week.

Only three kinds of urgency exist. Decide which one each new thing is.

UrgencyWhat you do
NowDrop everything and deal with it. Don’t write it down. This is rare.
TodayWrite it on today’s page, under the line. Use this sparingly.
TomorrowLift the page and write it on tomorrow’s. This is the default.

Five marks

Put a mark at the start of every line. An open dot is the only thing that still needs you.

MarkMeaningHow
•TaskA dot before the words
×DoneDraw the × over the dot. The words stay readable.
 abc DroppedA line through the words. Dropping is a decision, and it counts.
›MovedOver the dot, then write where: “› Sat”, “› Later”, “› Someday”
–NoteA fact worth keeping: “– quote was $180”

Optional sixth mark: ○ for an appointment or event. Use it if you keep times in the notebook. Skip it if your phone calendar does that job.

That’s all. Ideas are just notes: copy the good ones to a back list during the evening sort.

One day on paper

Follow Friday from the night before to Saturday morning. Watch where new things go.

Moved three times? Change it. Break it into a smaller first step, give it a date on the Later page, or put a line through it. A task you keep moving is a decision you haven’t made yet.

The evening sort: Defer, Act, File, Toss

Read down today’s page. Every open dot gets one of four verbs. It takes two minutes.

VerbWhenMark
ActIt takes two minutes. Do it now.×
DeferStill matters soon. Move it to tomorrow’s page, or to Later with a date.›
FileMatters, but not this week. Move it to a back list: Now, Someday or Waiting for.›
TossNo longer matters. abc 

Then write one line at the bottom of the page: your energy out of 10 and one word for the day. It takes ten seconds and, after a month, shows you what good days have in common.

Lists at the back

Turn the notebook over and start from the back. These lists outlast any single day. Most people need two of them. Few need all four.

Extras go here too, one page each, only when you need them: a habit grid (one row per habit, one box per day), a spend log, a list of books and films, or a countdown plan. For the countdown plan, write the deadline at the bottom of the page and work upward to today, one step per line. Each step gets its own small deadline, with a rest built in.

Look back, in three sizes

Reflection only sticks if it’s small. Each of these fits in the time you have.

Every evening · 10 seconds

One line

Energy out of 10 and one word, at the bottom of the day page. With the 30-minute tally extra, add a stroke for each half hour of real focus.

Every week · 10 minutes

The review

The checklist below, finishing with three short answers on the Weeks page at the back.

Every month · 5 minutes

Prune

Cut the Someday list. Drop any extra you’ve stopped using. Ask whether everything on Now still deserves a place.

Weekly review

Pick a regular time, such as Sunday evening.

  • Flip through the week. Give every plain dot a verb: Act, Defer, File or Toss.
  • Look two weeks ahead on the Later page or your calendar. Copy anything due onto the right day page.
  • Check Now: five projects or fewer, each with a next step.
  • Chase anything overdue on Waiting for.
  • On the Weeks page, write how many days you finished your list, one thing that worked, one thing that kept getting moved, and one thing to stop.
  • Choose next week’s focus.

Count finished days. Finishing the list four days out of five is a good week. Reward it. The score tells you whether your lists are the right size.

When you fall behind

Every paper system gets dropped for a few days at some point. What counts is how cheap it is to come back.

Missed some days

Don’t fill in the gap. Date today’s page, copy over only what still matters, and carry on. The old pages can wait for the weekly review.

Can’t finish the list

Three unfinished days in a row means something needs fixing. Either you’re doing a lot that isn’t on the list, or you’ve agreed to too much, or you haven’t left time for ordinary life. Cut commitments before you cut sleep.

Stuck on one task

Write the smallest start: “just find the folder”. Commit to 30 minutes and allow yourself to stop after that. Before a break, start the next thing, so coming back is easy.

Set up in two minutes

  1. Label the cover with a phone number or email and the start date. Pocket notebooks get lost.
  2. Copy the card from the end of this guide onto the inside cover.
  3. Write day pages on the front of each sheet only. Tomorrow then stays one lift away, and any page can be torn out without losing something on its back.
  4. Skip the index. The date is the address. Anything worth finding again belongs in a list at the back.
  5. Decide where dates live: your phone calendar or the Later page. Pick one.
  6. Leave the back empty until a trigger tells you to start a list.

The notebook is full when the day pages reach the lists at the back. Copy anything still open onto the first page of the next notebook, carry over the back lists you still use, and write the end date on the cover.

Using the spiral

The wire binding is what makes a flip notebook different from a bound book. Use it on purpose.

Hand someone a note

Write it on the next blank sheet, tear it out and hand it over. On today’s page, write “› note to Jo” so you know where it went.

Keep private things private

Use a sheet of its own and tear it out when you’re done. Shred it if it matters. Keep passwords somewhere else altogether.

Keep a clean working page

Flip finished days behind the cover. Today is always on top, and tomorrow is underneath.

Tear without ripping

Crease the page flat against the wire first, then pull. Cheap paper tears unevenly if you pull straight out.

Keep what matters. If a torn-out page holds something worth keeping, photograph it before you throw it away.

No spiral? Make a stapled one

Cut 6 sheets of A4 in half to get 12 pieces of A5. Fold each in half, nest them inside a folded card cover, and staple twice through the fold. That gives 48 pages of about 105 × 148 mm, the size of a Field Notes book. Round the corners so it slides into a pocket. In a stapled book, tomorrow is simply the next page. Keep tear-out notes on a loose slip tucked in the back.
